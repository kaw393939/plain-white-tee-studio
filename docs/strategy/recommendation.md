# Our next move: start with a moment

**Prototype First Layer first:** a plain white tee positioned as a simple starting point for a student entering a new, informal setting. Build a small, useful page that helps them prepare and express their own style. Treat this as an experiment, with Dayform and Common Ground available as alternatives.

This is a recommendation about what to test, not a chosen brand or a claim of proven demand. The [competitive examples](/research/landscape) show several established ways to sell a basic. They do not establish that this proposed position is unoccupied.

## Why this direction earns a prototype

“Premium essentials” would invite comparison with the fit and construction details shown by ASKET and Buck Mason. “Responsible basics” would require product-specific evidence comparable to the claims published by Organic Basics and Everybody.World. A low-price promise would be judged against multipack economics such as Hanes. See the [linked observations and limitations](/research/sources).

We have an unchanged classroom product and no verified material, supply-chain, durability, or performance advantages. We can, however, test a specific wearing situation and a useful editorial service. First Layer offers a clear starting task: **help someone decide whether and how the same white tee belongs in an upcoming informal occasion.**

Dayform could accomplish something similar for everyday routine. Common Ground could become compelling through real creative participation. Start with one situation so we can tell which message people understand.

## A proposed brief

| Decision | First prototype hypothesis |
| --- | --- |
| Primary visitor | A student preparing for a first creative club meeting, studio session, or other informal new setting |
| Need | Feel prepared while keeping personal expression and respecting the setting |
| Working name | First Layer — provisional, availability and trademark clearance not checked |
| Working promise | Start with a blank. Show up as yourself. |
| Brand character | Everyperson: approachable and reassuring |
| Voice | Direct, warm, understated |
| Useful content | Three occasion-aware outfit suggestions and a freely available preparation checklist |
| Secondary visitor | Someone already comfortable with the setting who wants a simple base for their own style |

A T-shirt is not suitable for every occasion. Give the visitor a useful reminder to check the actual dress expectations. Avoid promising confidence, acceptance, or success from a purchase.

## The smallest useful experiment

1. **Listen first.** Speak with five to eight plausible wearers. Ask about the last time they wore or bought a white tee, a new setting they prepared for, and the questions they actually had. This is a small discovery exercise, not a representative survey. No interviews have been completed for this site.
2. **Compare messages.** Make equal-depth concept cards for [Dayform, Common Ground, and First Layer](/strategy/ideas), using the identical garment and consistent image quality. Ask who each seems intended for and what it offers. Ask for reasons before explaining the intended message.
3. **Choose a provisional brief.** Favor a direction people understand and connect to an actual need. Revise or abandon First Layer if it misses that test. Record the reason on the [decision board](/project/decisions).
4. **Compare two hero designs for that brief.** Hold the product, headline, supporting copy, price treatment, and action identical. Compare a researched modernist treatment with a researched postmodernist treatment. The [source brief](/project/brief) explains this controlled comparison.
5. **Build one coherent slice.** Apply the chosen direction to a storefront and live style guide. Preserve the size selector and temporary demo bag from the original project when implementation begins. Test behavior and ask people to describe the meaning they actually see.

The three concept cards test positioning. The later two hero designs test presentation within one brief. Mixing those questions in a single comparison would make the result harder to interpret.

## What would make us revise?

| Observation to collect | Practical response |
| --- | --- |
| People cannot tell which situation First Layer serves | Narrow the occasion and rewrite the page before polishing the visuals |
| The framing creates anxiety or sounds like a promise of social approval | Remove that pressure; keep preparation advice freely useful |
| People value everyday simplicity more than a “new beginning” | Explore Dayform next |
| People want to share their actual creative work and grant permission | Explore Common Ground with real contributions |
| People mainly ask about fit, opacity, shrinkage, or price | Treat those as research and product-information gaps; do not answer with invented specifications |
| People prefer their current shirt or secondhand options | Preserve that finding; investigate whether a new product is needed |

These are qualitative decision rules, not fabricated results or numerical success thresholds.

## Persuasion with a useful purpose

**Reciprocity, proposed:** give the preparation checklist to everyone without an email gate or purchase requirement. It offers help before asking for anything.

**Liking, proposed:** use welcoming, ordinary language and original, permissioned styling examples so visitors can assess whether the brand relates to their situation. Do not fabricate relatable customer stories or describe this as a measured conversion improvement.

The source exercise asks for two truthful applications. These remain proposals until a direction and actual implementation are chosen. See its [ethical persuasion guidance](https://github.com/kaw393939/is117-brand-studio/blob/ca4b26431310a419e7143c56cacbc450c452879b/docs/reference.md#persuasion-that-respects-a-visitor).

## Keep the product facts separate

The source storefront’s **$28 USD is a fictional demo price**, not our recommended retail price. Competitor prices vary by pack, product, sale, region, and selected variant. They cannot establish our costs, willingness to pay, or margin. Keep the teaching price visibly fictional until there is an explicit decision and supporting basis.

Do not publish claims about fabric, sustainability, origin, opacity, fit, shipping, returns, stock, endorsements, or customer counts without the relevant evidence. A brand promise can guide a prototype; it cannot substitute for product proof.
